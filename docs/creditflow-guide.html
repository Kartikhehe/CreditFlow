01

What this project actually is

Imagine you run a company that lends money to people. Someone applies for a $15,000 loan. You have to answer one question: do I give this person money or not?

Say yes to the wrong person and they stop paying — you lose most of that $15,000. Say no to the right person and you lose the interest they would have paid you. Both mistakes cost money. CreditFlow is a system that makes this decision as well as possible, and — just as importantly — can explain every decision afterwards.

It does five things:

  1. Collects and organises the data. It takes roughly 30 million historical loan applications plus economic data (unemployment rates, interest rates) and arranges them into a clean, queryable database.
  2. Predicts risk. It builds two models that estimate the probability that any given applicant will stop paying. One model is simple and explainable; the other is more accurate but harder to explain. The project deliberately uses the simpler one as the real model and keeps the complex one as a benchmark.
  3. Turns a prediction into a decision. A probability isn't an answer. The system works out the money maths — revenue, losses, funding costs — and finds the approval threshold that makes the most profit.
  4. Watches for the model going stale. If the kind of people applying today differs from the people the model learned from, the model's predictions become untrustworthy. The system measures this and raises a flag.
  5. Answers questions in plain English. There's an AI assistant you can ask "what's the default rate by credit score band?" and it writes the database query, runs it safely, and shows you the answer. It also drafts regulatory documentation.
The thing that makes this project stand out

Most portfolio projects build a model and report an accuracy number. This one builds the entire chain around the model: where the data came from, whether the data is trustworthy, whether the model is allowed to be used, what business decision it drives, and what happens when it degrades. That chain is what an actual lending institution needs, and it's what separates a student project from something resembling production work.

The "Lending Club" story behind the data

Lending Club was a US company that connected borrowers to investors. Because investors needed to judge the risk themselves, Lending Club published detailed records of every loan it funded — the borrower's credit profile, the loan terms, and crucially what eventually happened (fully repaid, or charged off). It also published the applications it rejected.

That second file is unusual and it's the reason this dataset is worth the effort. Almost every public credit dataset only shows you approved loans. Having the rejected ones lets this project tackle a genuinely hard statistical problem (explained in reject inference) that most treatments ignore entirely.

02

Every finance term, in plain words

These are the terms you'll see across the code, the docs, and this manual. Learn these and the project becomes readable.

The basics of lending

Loan origination

The moment a loan is created and the money is handed over. "Origination date" = the day the loan started. "At origination" = information known on that day, before anyone knew whether it would be repaid.

Principal

The actual amount of money borrowed, not counting interest. Borrow $10,000 and the principal is $10,000.

Interest rate / coupon

The percentage charged per year for borrowing. A $10,000 loan at 13% costs you $1,300 a year in interest. This is the lender's revenue. "Coupon" is just industry slang for the interest rate on a specific loan.

Term

How long the loan lasts. In this dataset, either 36 months or 60 months.

Instalment

The fixed monthly payment the borrower makes, covering both principal and interest.

Default / charge-off

The borrower stopped paying. "Charged off" is the accounting term for when the lender formally gives up on collecting and writes the loan off as a loss. In this project, default is the thing we're trying to predict. It's recorded as is_default = 1.

Fully paid

The borrower repaid everything. is_default = 0. This is the good outcome.

Terminal status

The loan has finished — it either fully paid or charged off. Loans still being repaid right now are "in flight" and we don't know how they'll end. The project only trains on terminal loans, because an unfinished loan has no known answer. This is tracked by the is_terminal flag.

Measuring the borrower

FICO score / credit score

A number from roughly 300 to 850 summarising how reliably someone has repaid debt in the past. Higher is better. 300–579 is poor, 800+ is excellent. It's the single most important predictor in consumer lending.

DTI — Debt-to-Income ratio

Monthly debt payments divided by monthly income, as a percentage. A DTI of 35% means 35 cents of every dollar you earn already goes to debt. Higher DTI means less room to absorb a shock, so higher risk.

Revolving utilisation

How much of your available credit card limit you're actually using. If you have $10,000 of limits and $9,000 of balance, utilisation is 90% — a strong signal of financial stress. Low utilisation suggests you have slack.

Delinquency

A missed or late payment, short of a full default. "Delinquencies in the last 2 years" counts recent slip-ups.

Credit inquiries

Each time you apply for credit, a lender checks your file and leaves an "inquiry". Many inquiries in 6 months can mean someone is desperately shopping for money — a risk signal.

Public records / bankruptcies

Legal financial events on your record. A bankruptcy is the strongest negative mark in consumer credit.

Grade and sub-grade

Lending Club's own risk rating, A (safest) through G (riskiest), with sub-grades like B3. Lending Club set the interest rate from this grade. This project uses the grade both as an input feature and, via its price, as a benchmark to beat.

Risk modelling terms

PD — Probability of Default

The model's output: a number between 0 and 1 saying how likely this borrower is to default. 0.08 means an 8% chance. This is the core prediction the entire system is built around.

LGD — Loss Given Default

If they do default, what fraction of the money do you actually lose? Not 100% — you can chase the debt and recover something. This project assumes 65%, meaning you lose 65 cents per dollar on a defaulted loan. These loans are unsecured (no house or car to repossess), so recovery is limited.

EAD — Exposure at Default

How much money is at risk. Here it's simply the funded loan amount.

Expected loss

The famous credit risk formula: Expected Loss = PD × LGD × EAD. A $10,000 loan with an 8% default chance and 65% loss severity has an expected loss of 0.08 × 0.65 × $10,000 = $520. That's the amount you should expect to lose on average, and it's what gets compared against the interest revenue.

Scorecard

The traditional, regulator-friendly credit model. Each borrower attribute earns a fixed number of points — 620–660 FICO might be worth +12 points, DTI above 30% might be −8 — and the total converts to a probability. Its advantage is total transparency: you can print it on one page and point at exactly why someone was declined.

WOE — Weight of Evidence

The technique for turning a raw number into scorecard points. You chop a variable (say FICO) into bands, and for each band compute how much more likely bad outcomes are versus good ones. The result is a single number per band expressing "how much evidence of risk does this band carry". It makes wildly different variables comparable and handles non-linear relationships.

IV — Information Value

A score for how predictive a variable is overall. Rough reading: below 0.02 is useless, 0.1–0.3 is medium, above 0.5 is strong. This project only admits variables with IV between 0.02 and 1.0 — the upper cap is a safety check, because a variable too predictive is usually secretly leaking the answer.

Monotonicity

The constraint that the relationship must only go one direction. A better FICO score must never increase predicted risk. Real data has noise that can suggest a bump where 700–720 looks riskier than 680–700; a monotonic model refuses to learn that bump. This matters legally — you cannot defend a policy that penalises someone for having a better credit score.

Champion and challenger

The champion is the model actually used for decisions. The challenger is an alternative run alongside to see if it would do better. In this project, the simple scorecard is champion and the more accurate machine learning model is challenger — a deliberate choice explained in the interview section.

How model quality is measured

AUC and Gini

AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) measures ranking ability: pick a random defaulter and a random good payer — what's the chance the model scored the defaulter as riskier? 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. Credit models typically score 0.65–0.72. Gini is just a rescaling: Gini = 2 × AUC − 1. Europe quotes Gini, the US quotes AUC; they say the same thing.

KS — Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic

The maximum gap between the cumulative distributions of good and bad borrowers. In practice: the single cutoff point where the model best separates the two groups. Higher is better.

Brier score

Measures whether the probabilities are numerically right, not just well-ordered. Lower is better. This matters because the profit calculation multiplies by the probability — see calibration.

Calibration

A model is calibrated if, among all applicants it scored at 10%, roughly 10% actually default. A model can rank perfectly but be badly calibrated — imagine it says 20% whenever the truth is 10%. The ranking (AUC) is flawless but every profit calculation is wrong. This project fixes this with isotonic regression, a technique that adjusts the probability levels while leaving the ordering untouched.

Discrimination

Industry jargon for ranking ability (AUC/Gini/KS). Nothing to do with unfair treatment — it means "can the model tell the two groups apart".

Portfolio and decision terms

Vintage

All loans originated in the same period, grouped together — like a wine vintage. "The 2015-Q3 vintage" means every loan issued in that quarter. Vintages are compared because economic conditions differ by year.

Months on book

How long a loan has existed. Essential for fair comparison: a loan issued 6 months ago has had almost no chance to default yet, while a 4-year-old loan has had every chance. Comparing their default rates directly is meaningless.

Vintage curve

The cumulative default rate plotted against months on book, one line per vintage. This lets you compare cohorts at the same age — "at 18 months, the 2015 vintage had defaulted at 6% while 2016 had reached 8%" — which is the only honest comparison.

Cutoff

The threshold. "Approve everyone with a PD at or below 15%." Moving the cutoff up approves more people and earns more interest but takes more losses. Finding the best cutoff is the whole decisioning problem.

Approval rate

The percentage of applicants you approve at a given cutoff.

Profit frontier

The curve of expected profit plotted against approval rate. Its peak is the profit-maximising cutoff. The project emphasises that this curve is usually flat near the top, meaning several approval rates are nearly equally good — so the final choice is a business appetite decision, not a mathematical one.

Swap set analysis

The comparison a credit committee actually asks for. Hold the number of approvals identical between two models, then look at the difference in who they approve. "Swap-ins" are people the new model approves that the old one rejected; "swap-outs" are the reverse. If the swap-ins default less than the swap-outs, the new model is genuinely ranking better. Holding volume equal removes the confound where a model just approves more people and looks different for boring reasons.

Cost of funds

What it costs the lender to obtain the money it lends out. Banks borrow at say 3.5% and lend at 13%; the gap is where profit lives. Assumed at 3.5% annually here.

Opex — operating expense

The cost of acquiring and servicing a loan: marketing, underwriting staff, payment processing, collections. Assumed at a flat $120 per booked loan.

Prepayment

The borrower repays early. Good for risk, bad for revenue — you don't collect the remaining interest. It concentrates among the best borrowers, who refinance elsewhere when rates improve. The project applies a flat 55% retention haircut to interest revenue and is openly critical of that flatness, since prepayment isn't uniform.

Through-the-door population

Everyone who applies, as opposed to everyone who gets approved. The distinction is the heart of the reject inference problem below.

Three concepts worth extra attention

Reject inference — the sample bias problem

Your historical data only has outcomes for loans you approved. But you approved those people precisely because they looked good. So your data describes "the default rate among people we approved", not "the default rate among people who apply".

Why this bites: if you loosen your policy to approve more people, you face a population your model has never described. Your metrics were measured on a filtered, friendlier sample.

How this project handles it: it scores the rejected applications with a deliberately stripped-down scorecard using only the five attributes both files share. Then — crucially — it does not label them as defaults. It carries each rejected applicant forward as a fraction: if inferred PD is 0.3, that person counts as 0.3 of a bad outcome and 0.7 of a good one. This is called parcelling or fuzzy augmentation. Nothing is pretended to be known.

The honest part: this rests on a "rejection penalty" — the assumption that rejected people are worse than approved people at the same score, because the original underwriter saw something the five shared columns don't capture. That multiplier cannot be derived from the data. So the project reports results across a range of penalties (1.0 to 2.0) instead of defending one, and lets the reader pick.

PSI — Population Stability Index

A number measuring how much the applicant population has shifted between when the model was built and now. Technically it's a distributional distance; practically it's "are today's applicants still the same kind of people the model learned from?"

Reading the result: below 0.10 stable 0.10–0.25 investigate above 0.25 revalidate

The implementation detail that matters: the bin boundaries are computed once on the original development data and then frozen. If you recompute the bins for each new period, both distributions get forced onto their own shapes and the index returns near-zero no matter how far things actually moved. Getting this wrong is the standard PSI bug, and this project has a test specifically asserting it fires on a genuinely shifted sample.

The score-level row matters most. Individual features can drift and cancel each other out. But if the model score distribution has moved, then the population being approved has changed — which is a policy change nobody signed off on.

Data leakage — the silent project-killer

Leakage is when your model accidentally sees information that wouldn't exist at decision time. The classic example here: using total_payment as a feature. Of course it predicts default perfectly — people who defaulted paid less! But at application time, that number doesn't exist. Your model shows 0.99 AUC in testing and is useless in production.

This project has five separate defences against it, listed in the pipeline section. The most explicit is a hard-coded BANNED_COLUMNS list and an assertion that crashes the build if any of those columns reaches the model. Knowing what leakage is and designing against it deliberately is one of the strongest signals of real experience you can show in an interview.

03

Every AI and GenAI term

The last part of the project is an AI assistant layer. These are the concepts behind it.

LLM — Large Language Model

The technology behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. A model trained on enormous amounts of text that can read instructions and generate text in response. Here it's used for two jobs: writing SQL queries from English questions, and drafting a regulatory document.

Prompt / system prompt

The instructions you send the model. A "system prompt" is the standing briefing — in this project it contains the database schema and the rules ("only write SELECT statements, never DDL"). The user's question is sent separately.

Hallucination

When an LLM confidently states something false. The reason this project is built the way it is: in a regulatory document, a hallucinated test result isn't a typo, it's a compliance incident. A supervisor reads that document as a statement of fact.

RAG — Retrieval Augmented Generation

The standard technique for stopping hallucination. Instead of asking the model to recall facts from training, you retrieve relevant real documents first and hand them to the model, instructing it to answer only from those. "Retrieval" = finding the right passages. "Augmented" = adding them to the prompt. "Generation" = the model writes the answer.

Grounding

Forcing the model's output to rest on supplied evidence. This project grounds twice over: regulatory claims must cite a retrieved guidance passage by name, and every number must come from the metric CSV files the pipeline produced. The prompt explicitly instructs the model to say "the evidence is insufficient" rather than invent something.

Chunking

Splitting long documents into passages small enough to retrieve and fit in a prompt. The subtlety here: this project splits on section boundaries first, then by length. A naive fixed-width split can separate a rule from the clause that qualifies it — producing an answer that's fluent, properly sourced, and completely wrong, because the retrieved passage said "banks must" without the next sentence's exemption.

Embedding / vector

A way of turning text into a list of numbers that captures its meaning. Texts with similar meanings get similar number-lists, so you can find related passages by mathematical closeness. This catches paraphrase — a query about "monitoring expectations" matches a passage about "ongoing performance review" even with no shared words.

Vector database

A database built to store embeddings and answer "find me the most similar ones, fast". This project uses ChromaDB.

Cosine similarity

The standard measure of how close two embeddings are. Think "angle between two arrows" — small angle means similar meaning.

BM25 / sparse retrieval

Classic keyword search, scoring documents by term overlap while discounting common words. It's the opposite strength to embeddings: it catches exact terminology. Regulatory text turns on precise phrases, and an embedding model may treat two technically distinct phrases as near-identical where a practitioner would not.

Hybrid retrieval

Running both dense (embedding) and sparse (BM25) search and combining the results. You get paraphrase tolerance and exact-term precision.

RRF — Reciprocal Rank Fusion

How this project merges the two result lists. The problem: cosine similarity scores and BM25 scores are on completely incomparable scales, and normalising them needs calibration that breaks whenever you add a document. RRF sidesteps this by ignoring scores entirely and using only rank position — each result contributes 1 / (60 + position), summed across both lists. Elegant and robust.

Cross-encoder / reranker

A second, slower, more accurate model applied to the shortlist. An embedding model reads the query and the passage separately, so it can only judge whether they're in a similar region of meaning-space. A cross-encoder reads both together and judges relevance directly — far more precise at picking which of twenty plausible passages actually answers the question. It's too slow to run over the whole corpus, so it reranks the top 20.

Text-to-SQL

Translating an English question into a database query. "What's the default rate by FICO band?" becomes a real SQL SELECT statement, which is executed and the result returned.

AST — Abstract Syntax Tree

The structured, tree-shaped representation of parsed code. Instead of treating SQL as text, you parse it into a tree where you can ask structural questions: "is the root node a SELECT?", "does any DELETE node appear anywhere beneath?", "what tables are referenced?"

Guardrail

A safety check between the AI's output and the real world. Here, never trust generated SQL. The guardrail parses it to an AST and requires: root must be SELECT, no mutating node anywhere in the tree, every table must be on an explicit allowlist, and a row limit is injected if missing. The database connection is also opened read-only, so even a guardrail bug can't cause a write.

Why a keyword blocklist was rejected — a great interview point

The naive guardrail is "reject any SQL containing DROP or DELETE". This fails in both directions. It's bypassable: comments, odd whitespace, string literals and nested statements can all hide a forbidden operation from a substring scan. And it produces false positives: a legitimate query like WHERE purpose = 'debt_consolidation' gets rejected because some reason code contains a banned word.

Parsing to an AST fixes both. The structure is what gets checked, so hiding is impossible, and a forbidden word appearing in a value is structurally distinct from a forbidden operation. The test suite has cases in both directions.

Execution accuracy

How the text-to-SQL benchmark is graded. You don't compare the generated SQL text against a reference — two competent analysts write different SQL for the same question, and string comparison would mark a correct answer wrong for choosing a different join order. Instead you run both and compare the result sets.

Refusal testing

Three of the 25 benchmark questions are designed to be unanswerable — one ambiguous, one outside the schema, one reachable only through a blocked table. They count as correct when the agent declines. A benchmark of only answerable questions measures fluency and tells you nothing about whether the safety layer works.

Where the data comes from

Three sources, none of which ship with the code. All are free.

1. Lending Club loan extracts (the main dataset)

Downloaded from Kaggle. Two CSV files arrive:

FileContainsShapeHas outcomes?
accepted_2007_to_2018Q4.csvFunded loans: borrower credit profile, loan terms, and final status~2.2M rows, ~150 columnsYes
rejected_2007_to_2018Q4.csvDeclined applications: date, amount, risk score, DTI, employment, state~27M rows, 9 columnsNo

That asymmetry — wide-with-labels versus huge-but-narrow-and-unlabelled — shapes the whole project. The accepted file is the only source of truth about who defaults, but it's a filtered sample. The rejected file is the other side of the decision, with no outcomes because none of them was funded.

The five columns both files share are what make reject inference possible at all: credit score, debt-to-income, employment length, state, and amount requested.

Reading detail worth mentioning in an interview

Schema inference is switched off when reading these CSVs, and every column is cast explicitly. Lending Club changed its column formatting over the eleven years it published. If you let a CSV reader guess the type from the first few thousand rows, you get columns that silently truncate or null out further down the file — a bug that produces no error and quietly corrupts your results.

2. FRED macroeconomic data

FRED is the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's public economic database. Free API key. The project pulls:

  • National series: unemployment rate, 10-year Treasury yield, consumer sentiment, consumer price index (inflation), and credit card delinquency rates.
  • Per-state series: unemployment rate for the 15 states with the most loan volume.

Why bother? Two identical borrowers, one who borrowed in 2007 just before the financial crisis and one in 2013 during recovery, will default at different rates — and it's not their fault. A model with no economic input blames the borrower for the economy. State-level unemployment matters more than national, because a borrower faces their local labour market.

Point-in-time correctness — a senior-level detail

Each loan is attached to the economic conditions of its own origination month, joined on exact month equality. Not a nearest match, not a forward-fill, not an as-of join that could reach ahead. A loan originated in March 2015 carries March 2015's unemployment rate and nothing later.

And the test for it doesn't just check the join condition — it independently recomputes the expected value from the month key and fails the build on any mismatch. A test written as a check on the join would pass for whatever join the code happened to contain. Recomputing means the test states the requirement and the code has to satisfy it.

3. Supervisory guidance PDFs (for the AI layer)

You manually place 4–6 regulatory PDFs in data/docs/, totalling 100–200 pages. These are published openly by their issuing bodies. The project was built against:

  • Federal Reserve SR 11-7 — guidance on model risk management. The foundational document for how banks must validate models.
  • Basel Committee — principles for the management of credit risk.
  • OCC Comptroller's Handbook — retail credit examination material.

These become the knowledge base the AI cites when drafting the validation memo. One document would make retrieval trivial and prove nothing about ranking quality, hence four to six.

Requirement

The PDFs must have a real text layer. Scanned images produce no extractable text, and the index build deliberately raises an error rather than writing an empty index — failing loudly beats silently building something useless.

05

Every technology and why it's there

For each tool: what it is, what job it does here, and why it was chosen over the obvious alternative. That last column is what interviewers actually probe.

Data engineering

Polars — CSV to Parquet conversion

What it is: A fast dataframe library (like pandas, but written in Rust).

Job here: Reading the enormous raw CSVs and writing them out as clean Parquet files.

Why not pandas: Polars has a lazy scanner that streams the file in chunks, so peak memory stays flat no matter how big the input. Pandas would need the entire multi-gigabyte file resident in RAM before it could write anything. On the 27-million-row rejected file, that's the difference between working and crashing.

Parquet — the storage format

What it is: A columnar file format. Instead of storing row by row, it stores column by column.

Why it matters: If your query only needs 3 of 150 columns, a columnar format reads only those 3. It also compresses far better, because similar values sit together. The project uses zstd compression.

Partitioning: Files are split into directories by origination year. Every query in this project filters on origination time, so DuckDB can skip entire directories rather than scanning eleven years of history to answer a question about one cohort. This is called partition pruning.

DuckDB — the warehouse

What it is: An analytical database that runs inside your own process, as a single file. Think "SQLite, but built for analytics".

Job here: The SQL engine everything queries. It reads the Parquet files directly, registered as views.

Why not Postgres or Snowflake: DuckDB reads Parquet in place and pushes filters down into the file scan, so registering the data costs nothing and no copy is made. A server-based warehouse would add an operational dependency — a service to install, run, and manage — to a project that runs on one laptop. And since they're views not tables, materialising a copy would waste several gigabytes for zero query-time benefit.

dbt — transformation and testing

What it is: "Data Build Tool". You write SQL SELECT statements in files; dbt works out the dependency order, runs them, and manages them as a graph. It also runs data-quality tests.

Job here: Transforming raw data into clean staging views, then into the final star schema. And running all the data quality tests.

Why it matters: The tests live next to the models they constrain and run in the same command. A broken assumption fails the build immediately, rather than surfacing three stages later as a weird model metric that takes a day to trace back.

Modelling

optbinning — WOE binning

What it is: A specialist library for optimal binning and scorecard construction.

Job here: Chopping each variable into bands, computing Weight of Evidence, and enforcing the monotonicity constraint. Also computes Information Value and does feature selection.

Why not hand-rolled: Writing binning yourself is easy; getting the monotonicity constraint right is not — and that constraint is precisely the part that makes a scorecard legally defensible. optbinning solves it as a proper constrained optimisation.

scikit-learn — logistic regression and calibration

What it is: The standard general-purpose machine learning library for Python.

Job here: Two things. The logistic regression that sits on top of the WOE-transformed features and forms the scorecard. And the isotonic regression that calibrates the challenger's probabilities.

Why logistic regression: Over WOE-transformed inputs it keeps each attribute's point contribution directly readable, which is the entire point of a scorecard.

LightGBM — the challenger model

What it is: A gradient boosting library. It builds hundreds of small decision trees in sequence, each correcting the previous ones' errors.

Job here: The challenger — the "what's the best achievable accuracy?" benchmark.

Why this one: It handles categorical variables natively (no manual encoding) and fits the full training window in minutes. Cheap enough to rerun the whole champion-challenger comparison whenever an assumption changes.

SHAP — challenger explanation

What it is: A method from cooperative game theory for attributing a prediction across its input features. "This prediction was 0.18; FICO pushed it down 0.04, DTI pushed it up 0.07."

Job here: Explaining the challenger, since a boosted ensemble is otherwise opaque.

The point being made: SHAP is the closest available answer to "why did the model score this applicant that way" — it approximates what the scorecard gives you exactly. That gap is precisely the trade-off the champion-challenger setup is measuring.

Retrieval and generation

ChromaDB — vector store

What it is: An embedded vector database.

Job here: Storing embeddings of the guidance document chunks and answering similarity searches. Uses HNSW indexing with cosine distance.

Why this one: Runs in-process with local file persistence — same philosophy as DuckDB, no server to operate.

sentence-transformers — embeddings and reranking

What it is: A library of pre-trained text embedding models.

Job here: Two models. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 turns chunks into embeddings. ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 is the cross-encoder that reranks the shortlist.

Note: These download automatically on first run (a few hundred MB) and then run entirely locally — no API cost.

rank-bm25 — keyword search

What it is: An implementation of the BM25 ranking function.

Job here: The sparse half of hybrid retrieval, catching exact regulatory terminology that embeddings blur.

sqlglot — the SQL guardrail

What it is: A SQL parser and transpiler. It turns SQL text into an inspectable syntax tree.

Job here: Validating every AI-generated query before it reaches the database.

Why not a blocklist: Covered above — a blocklist is both bypassable and prone to false positives. An AST check is structural and therefore neither.

Google Gemini (google-genai) — the LLM

What it is: Google's large language model, accessed through an API. The project uses gemini-3.5-flash-lite for text-to-SQL and gemini-3.6-flash for the memo, both on the free tier.

Job here: Two tasks. Writing SQL from English questions. And drafting the model validation memo from retrieved guidance plus measured metrics.

Note: This was originally built against Anthropic's Claude API and swapped to Gemini so it runs on a free key. The code structure is identical — one single-turn text generation call in each place — which is itself a point worth making: the LLM is a swappable component behind a small interface, not the architecture.

pypdf — PDF text extraction

What it is: A PDF manipulation library.

Job here: Pulling the text layer out of the guidance PDFs so they can be chunked and indexed.

Application and orchestration

Streamlit — the front end

What it is: A framework for building data web apps in pure Python, no HTML or JavaScript.

Job here: The dashboard. Multiple tabs showing vintage curves, model metrics, the profit frontier, stability monitoring, and the copilot.

Key design choice: Every tab reads generated artifacts from results/ rather than recomputing anything. Opening the app never triggers a model fit. And if an artifact doesn't exist yet, the app shows a one-line notice naming the make target that produces it — a stack trace on a fresh clone is a worse answer than an instruction.

Plotly — interactive charts

What it is: A charting library producing interactive (hover, zoom) charts.

Job here: The charts inside the Streamlit app.

matplotlib — static figures

What it is: The long-standing Python plotting library.

Job here: The PNG figures written to results/figures/ — the calibration curve, SHAP summary, and profit frontier. Static files that go in a report.

Dagster — orchestration

What it is: A data orchestrator. You declare "assets" (things that get produced) and their dependencies; it works out what to run and in what order.

Job here: An alternative to running make targets by hand, with a visual dependency graph and a web UI.

Why the layers are separated like this

Why is SQL (dbt) separate from Python (src)?
Set-based work — joining, aggregating, shaping tables — belongs in SQL, where a database engine optimises it. Row-wise and iterative work — fitting a model, sweeping thresholds — belongs in Python. Putting each in its natural place means dbt can run data-quality tests on the shaped data before any Python touches it.
Why does src/features/build.py exist separately from the marts?
Because the mart deliberately keeps the post-origination columns — the profitability analysis needs realised cash flows. The modelling frame must never see them. Having a distinct boundary with an assertion on it makes the separation explicit and enforceable, rather than relying on every future query remembering a WHERE clause.
Why is sql_guard.py a standalone module with no API client and no DB connection?
So the test suite can exercise the security logic directly, with no API key and no database. Security code you can't easily test is security code that doesn't get tested.
Why does paths.py exist?
Every output path derives from one constant per module. This makes a rule enforceable: scripts write to results/ and nowhere else. Which in turn makes two questions answerable — did this number come from the current code or a run three weeks ago, and does a fresh clone actually reproduce the work?
Why is results/ tracked as structure but not contents?
The repository tracks the code that produces results, not the results. A clone regenerates everything rather than inheriting someone else's output. This is a real discipline point: it makes make all the only way a result can come into existence.

The star schema, explained

The marts layer uses a star schema — the standard warehouse design. One central "fact" table holds the measurements, surrounded by "dimension" tables holding descriptive attributes. Drawn out, it looks like a star.

TableTypeOne row = Holds
fct_loanFactone funded loanAmounts, rate, term, default flag, cash flows, point-in-time macro
fct_application_funnelFactone applicationAccepted and declined stacked on shared columns
dim_borrowerDimensionone borrowerFICO, income, DTI, employment, utilisation
dim_geographyDimensionone stateState code, census region
dim_dateDimensionone monthYear, quarter, crisis-period flag

The grain point. "Grain" means what one row represents. fct_loan is at loan grain. fct_application_funnel is at application grain — a different, coarser thing, including applications that never became loans. Keeping them as separate tables rather than forcing them together is correct: mixing grains in one table is one of the most common and most damaging warehouse design errors, because every aggregate silently double-counts.

5a · Feature build and the temporal split

Pulls origination-time attributes only, runs the leakage assertion, engineers derived features, then splits by time:

SplitYearsUsed for
trainthrough 2015Fitting bins and coefficients
valid2016Early stopping, probability calibration
test2017 onwardHeld out entirely — final reporting

The engineered features are all things a lender could compute at application: loan-to-income, instalment burden, income versus state median (reads a salary against local cost of living rather than the national distribution), and macro stress (the gap between the borrower's state unemployment and the national rate, isolating local conditions from the cycle everyone faced).

Why temporal, not random — two reasons, the second more important

First: under a random split the model trains on 2018 loans while scoring 2016 loans. Production never offers that. A model deployed in 2016 has seen nothing after 2016, and any signal from the future leaks straight into the reported metric.

Second, and the one that matters more: a random split draws train and test from the same population by construction, so it cannot detect drift — the two samples are the same sample, shuffled. The stability report exists to measure how far the population moved between development and deployment. Against a random split, that measurement is guaranteed to return nothing regardless of what actually happened.

Note also that validation sits between train and test rather than being carved out of train. Early stopping and calibration both consult it, so both are tuned under the same forward-in-time gap the test window later measures.

The five leakage controls, in data-flow order

  1. Terminal-status gating. Only loans with a realised outcome enter the population, so the target is never a guess.
  2. Post-origination column exclusion. BANNED_COLUMNS names every field describing what happened after money moved. The assertion is a set intersection against the frame's actual columns, so it catches a column arriving through a renamed alias or an upstream select * widening — not just one someone adds by hand.
  3. Point-in-time macro attachment. Joined on exact month equality in the mart, enforced by independently recomputing the expected value.
  4. Binning fitted on train only. Fitting bin edges across the full frame would let the out-of-time distribution influence where the cuts fall — subtle enough to survive most review, and still a leak.
  5. Calibration fitted on validation, not test. Calibrating on the sample used to report calibration measures nothing.

5b · The champion scorecard

optbinning bins all 25 features with a monotonicity constraint, selects by Information Value (floor 0.02, cap 1.0, max 15 features), then logistic regression fits on the WOE-transformed inputs. Probabilities are also mapped to a points scale — 20 points to double the odds, anchored at 600 points for 50:1 good:bad odds, and decreasing in risk so higher score means safer, as every downstream reader expects.

The IV cap of 1.0 is a safety check: a variable too predictive is usually a proxy for the outcome.

5c · The challenger

LightGBM with early stopping watching the validation window, then isotonic regression calibrating the probabilities — fitted on validation, which is held out of tree fitting, so the calibrator isn't learning from its own residuals. SHAP explains it. Calibration curve and SHAP summary both get written as figures.

5d · Reject inference

A reduced scorecard is fitted on only the five shared columns, then used to score a 500,000-row sample of declined applications. Each is carried forward as fractional good and bad weights. The sensitivity table reports the through-the-door default rate across penalties 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2.0.

The reduced model is deliberately impoverished. Imputing the twenty missing attributes would manufacture the very signal the exercise is trying to measure. Its weaker discrimination is the honest ceiling: the inference is only as strong as what five shared columns can separate.

The unit economics

expected revenue = (1 − PD) × funded × rate × term_years × prepay_retention
expected loss    = PD × funded × LGD
funding cost     = funded × cost_of_funds × term_years
expected profit  = revenue − loss − funding cost − opex

With: LGD 0.65, cost of funds 3.5%, opex $120/loan, prepayment retention 0.95, average balance factor 0.55. Every one is a named constant in the code, because the conclusion moves with them and a reader needs to see what they'd have to disagree with.

The amortisation point — worth understanding properly

These are amortising instalment loans: the borrower repays principal every month, so the balance declines to zero across the term. The average balance outstanding is therefore roughly half the amount funded, not all of it.

That matters for all three legs of the calculation. Interest is earned on the declining balance, funding is paid on the declining balance, and a loan that defaults in month 20 has already repaid part of its principal, so the loss is on the balance at default. Charging any of them on full principal for the full term overstates it by about a factor of two.

And the errors do not cancel: funding cost is a straight subtraction while interest is scaled down by the survival probability. Get this wrong and the model reports that every cutoff loses money, which is how this bug announced itself.

How the assumptions were validated, not guessed

The 2012–2014 vintages are old enough that nearly every loan has terminated, so their realised net cash flow is a fact rather than a projection: +11.33% return on funded amount. Feeding those cohorts through the formula with perfect foresight (setting PD to the actual outcome) should land near that figure.

It gives +7.56% — conservative by about 3.8 percentage points, which is a defensible place to be for a decision model. An earlier version of the formula returned −0.23% on the same test, and that gap is what exposed the double-counting: a 0.55 prepayment haircut and a 0.55 balance factor were both charging for early termination.

This back-test against realised cash flow is the single most useful check in the whole decision layer, because it is the only part that needs no assumptions at all.

The cutoff is swept from 0.02 to 0.60 in steps of 0.005, reporting the whole frontier rather than one recommended number.

Predicted and realised bad rate both appear — deliberately

The gap between them at a given cutoff is a calibration check read at the point the decision is actually taken. For this purpose that's more informative than any aggregate calibration statistic, because it tells you whether the model is trustworthy exactly where it's being used.

The incumbent baseline

Comparing a new model against nothing isn't a comparison. The incumbent policy is proxied by the percentile rank of the priced interest rate — Lending Club set each coupon from its own grade assignment, so the rate ordering is the closest available stand-in for the risk ranking actually in force. It's a proxy with a known weakness (price reflects competitive positioning and funding conditions too), and it's the honest baseline available.

Stability monitoring

PSI computed per feature and on the model score, with bin edges frozen from the development sample. Answers to likely interview questions

Short, usable answers. Say these in your own words.

"Walk me through this project."

Thirty-second version: "It's an end-to-end credit decisioning platform on the Lending Club book. It ingests about 30 million applications plus macroeconomic data into a dimensional warehouse, builds a regulator-style scorecard as the deployed model with a gradient-boosted challenger as a benchmark, converts the probability into an approve/decline policy under stated unit economics, monitors population drift against the development sample, and exposes it through a Streamlit app plus a RAG-grounded copilot that answers questions in SQL and drafts validation documentation. The point of it was the chain around the model, not the model."

"Why is the less accurate model your champion?"

Because of what has to happen when an application is declined. The lender owes the applicant a reason and a validator a derivation. A monotonic WOE scorecard gives both by construction — each attribute contributes a fixed number of points, the decline reason is the attribute whose points fell furthest below neutral, and the whole model fits on one page. SHAP approximates that over an ensemble; it doesn't replace it, and in regulated lending the difference is legally material.

The scorecard also enforces monotonicity, so a better attribute value can never increase modelled risk. A tree ensemble will learn a locally non-monotone response wherever the training data suggests one, and you cannot defend a policy that penalises someone for a better credit score.

What the challenger contributes is the bound: it measures honestly how much discrimination the constrained form actually costs. That's a decision made with a number attached, not an assumption.

"How do you know you don't have leakage?"

Five controls. Terminal-status gating so the target is never a guess. An explicit banned-column list with an assertion that fails the build — implemented as a set intersection against actual columns, so it catches a renamed alias or an upstream select * widening, not just a hand-added column. Point-in-time macro joins enforced by a test that independently recomputes the expected value. Bin edges fitted on train only. And calibration fitted on validation, never test.

"Why temporal splits?"

Two reasons, and the second is the one people miss. First, a random split lets the model train on 2018 while scoring 2016, which production never offers. Second — and more important — a random split draws train and test from the same population by construction, so it cannot detect drift. They're the same sample, shuffled. Since the stability report exists specifically to measure how far the population moved, a random split would guarantee that measurement returned nothing no matter what actually happened.

"Why calibrate if AUC doesn't change?"

Because the decision engine multiplies predicted probability by exposure and LGD to get expected loss, then compares against revenue. That arithmetic needs a probability, not a ranking. A model reporting every probability at twice its true value has a flawless ordering and produces a wrong cutoff. Isotonic regression fixes the level while preserving order — so the discrimination metrics are untouched and the probabilities become usable. Fitted on validation, because calibrating on the sample you report calibration from measures nothing.

"What's the weakest part of this project?"

Good answer to have ready — the docs name several:

  • The flat prepayment haircut is the crudest piece. Prepayment concentrates in the strongest borrowers, exactly the population a tighter cutoff retains, so a flat factor understates revenue when loose and overstates when tight — biasing the recommended approval rate upward. Modelling prepayment as a function of credit quality would be the highest-value refinement.
  • Constant LGD at 0.65. A loan charging off in month four has repaid almost nothing; one at month thirty has returned a substantial fraction. A constant understates the first and overstates the second, and it's the assumption the optimal cutoff is most exposed to.
  • No correlated-loss modelling. The framework treats applications as independent, so it says nothing about a downturn hitting the whole book at once — which, given that the macro features exist because that correlation is real, is its most conspicuous gap.
  • Months on book is derived from last payment date, not a payment schedule. For a prepaid loan that measures when repayment finished, not how long the loan was contractually alive — so the vintage curves are cumulative incidence, not a hazard rate.
  • No roll-rate analysis, because the data can't support one: roll rates need month-by-month delinquency states and the extract publishes one terminal status per loan.

"How do you stop the AI from making things up?"

Grounding on two separate channels. Regulatory statements may only rest on retrieved guidance passages, each cited inline by source. Numbers may only come from the metric files the pipeline wrote. The prompt explicitly instructs the model to state that evidence is insufficient and say what additional testing would settle it, rather than fill the gap.

That instruction is the reason the module is built that way: an ungrounded model writing validation documentation doesn't fail by producing an awkward sentence, it fails by producing a confidently fabricated test result inside a document a supervisor reads as an assertion of fact. That's a compliance incident, not a software defect.

"Why not just block dangerous SQL keywords?"

Because it fails both ways. It's bypassable through comments, whitespace, string literals and nesting. And it produces false positives — it rejects a legitimate query that merely mentions a forbidden word in a value. So the statement is parsed to a syntax tree and the tree gets checked: root must be a SELECT, no mutating node anywhere beneath it, every table resolving to either a CTE in the same statement or a name on the allowlist, and a row limit injected if absent. The connection is read-only regardless, so a validator bug still can't write. The test suite has cases in both directions.

"What would you do next?"

  • Model prepayment as a function of credit quality — the highest-value fix to the decision framework.
  • Model LGD rather than fixing it, using time-on-book at default and outstanding balance.
  • Add a correlated-loss or stress-scenario layer, so the framework says something about a downturn hitting the whole book.
  • Add fairness testing across protected-characteristic proxies — a real regulatory requirement this doesn't currently address.
  • Expand the text-to-SQL benchmark and add adversarial prompt-injection cases to the guardrail suite.

If something breaks

The bugs already fixed — and the debugging method

Worth reading as stories, because the method matters more than the fixes. These are also good interview material: being able to describe how you cornered a silent hang says more than any feature you built.

1 · optbinning capped numpy below 2

The pins asked for numpy==2.1.3, but optbinning==0.19.0 declares numpy<2. Unsatisfiable, and pip said so plainly. Fixed by moving to optbinning==0.20.1, which drops the cap.

2 · dbt wrote tables to the wrong schema

make analysis failed with Table with name fct_loan does not exist — did you mean "main_marts.fct_loan"? dbt's default generate_schema_name concatenates the target schema with the custom one, so +schema: marts became main_marts, while every query in the project addresses marts directly.

Fixed with a generate_schema_name macro that uses the custom name verbatim — one 12-line macro, rather than editing the 11 files that reference marts.

3 · The two-hour silent hang — the instructive one

make model ran for 1h 55m with no output and no error. It looked like a slow model fit on 829,355 rows. It was not.

What gave it away: the process had used only 47 seconds of CPU across those two hours, at 1.3% utilisation, holding 4 MB of memory. A genuine fit on that data would sit near 100% CPU and hundreds of MB. Low CPU with flat, tiny memory means a process that is blocked, not working.

Three wrong guesses first: a 51-category variable (tested: 0.3s), a sparse count feature (it did stall, but a time limit "fixed" it in isolation while the full run still hung), and CPU contention from Spotlight indexing the 10 GB download (real, but the hang survived an idle machine).

What actually settled it: dumping the Python stack with faulthandler instead of guessing. It pointed straight at ortools/sat/python/cp_model.py in solve. Then one decisive test: a trivial CP-SAT model with max_time_in_seconds = 5.0 ran past 180 seconds. The solver was ignoring its own time limit, so no configuration could ever have fixed it.

The cause: ortools 9.10 is compiled against protobuf 5.26, but pip had installed protobuf 5.29 to satisfy dbt. The native extension loaded without complaint and then wedged inside Solve() — an ABI mismatch that fails by hanging rather than raising, which is the worst possible failure mode. Hence the two environments.

The transferable lesson: when something hangs, measure CPU and memory before theorising. Those two numbers distinguish "slow" from "stuck", and a stuck process needs a stack trace, not a faster algorithm.

4 · Every cutoff showed negative profit

The optimiser reported losses at all 116 cutoffs and picked the tightest one. The cause was in the unit economics, not the sweep: interest, funding cost and loss on default were all charged on the full principal for the full term, ignoring that these loans amortise. See the amortisation note in the decide stage.

How it was validated: by back-testing the formula against the seasoned 2012–14 vintages, whose realised cash flow is a fact rather than a projection. With perfect foresight the formula returned −0.23% where reality was +11.33%, which located the error precisely. It now returns +7.56% — conservative, and defensible.

5 · The Gemini model name was dead, and the quota is tiny

All 25 benchmark cases failed with 404 NOT_FOUND: gemini-2.0-flash had been retired, and gemini-2.5-flash turned out to be closed to new users. The installed SDK (google-genai 0.3.0) was too old to even list the available models.

Then a subtler one: the free tier allows 20 requests per day per model, and the benchmark needs 25. It could never pass on one model, and my first instinct — adding 4-second pacing — was solving a per-minute problem that did not exist. The quota is counted per model, so the fix was to point the benchmark and the memo at different models.

The lesson: a wall of identical API errors is an infrastructure signal, not a measurement. Reading 3/25 as "the agent is bad at SQL" would have been entirely wrong.

General debugging approach

  1. Read the error message. This codebase raises deliberately helpful errors — most name the exact make target or command that fixes the problem.
  2. Check the venv is active. Your prompt should show (.venv).
  3. Check prerequisites exist. Each stage needs the previous one's output. ls the expected file.
  4. Run the stage's script directly instead of through make, for a cleaner traceback: e.g. python -m src.features.build.
  5. Run make lint to parse every Python and SQL file — catches syntax errors fast.
  6. Run make clean to clear dbt's target/ and __pycache__. Safe; touches no data or results.
Nothing here is unrecoverable

Everything in data/, results/, models/ and chroma_db/ is regenerated from source. If a stage produces something wrong, delete its output and rerun — there's no hidden cached state to fight, which is exactly what the one-output-root discipline buys you.
